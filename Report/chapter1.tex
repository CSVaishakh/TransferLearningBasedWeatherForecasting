\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

Weather shapes daily life in Kerala more directly than in most places. The state receives two monsoons, its agriculture and water supply depend on their timing and intensity, and the Western Ghats produce sharp local differences in temperature and rainfall over distances of a few tens of kilometres. A forecast that is good for a district may therefore be poor for a particular campus, farm or catchment, and the places that need a local forecast are usually the places with the least local data to build one from.

At the same time, a great deal of useful local data already exists, but only on paper. Hawkins et al.\ show that rescuing even a modest number of historical paper observations changes what is known about past extreme weather \cite{ref1}. St.\ Joseph's College of Engineering and Technology (SJCET), Palai, is one such place: the college operates a manual weather station and its staff have recorded daily readings in a paper register for close to twenty years. This project digitizes that register and uses it, together with abundant regional weather data, to build a forecasting model for the campus through \textbf{staged transfer learning}.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

The SJCET register is read once a day at about 08:30 IST. Each row records the dry-bulb and wet-bulb temperatures, the maximum and minimum temperatures and the rainfall since the previous reading, and the observers also wrote monthly rainfall totals. The record is \textbf{handwritten}, with the corrections and overwritings of any long-running manual log; it is \textbf{short} by machine-learning standards, with 1,549 observed days in the clean scans of July 2010 to December 2016; and its monthly totals act as \textbf{checksums} that make a careful digitization verifiable. As long as the register stays on paper, it cannot be searched, analysed or used to train a model, and it deteriorates with time.

Data-driven forecasting has advanced rapidly. Models such as Pangu-Weather \cite{ref5} and FuXi \cite{ref6}, trained on decades of global reanalysis, now rival operational numerical weather prediction. These systems, however, depend on dense, gridded, globally consistent training data, which a single college weather station cannot provide. A network trained on a few years of one station's readings will either underfit or memorise, while a model trained only on regional data describes grid-cell averages rather than the conditions at the station itself.

Transfer learning bridges this gap by reusing what a model learns on a data-rich source task to improve a related, data-poor target task \cite{ref8}. Nipen et al.\ have shown that a graph neural network pretrained on global reanalysis and refined on a few years of regional data outperforms an operational regional forecast \cite{ref7}. This project carries the same idea one level further down, from region to station.

The \textbf{problem} addressed is therefore twofold: nearly twenty years of local observations at SJCET remain on paper, and the few years that can be digitized are too few to train an accurate local forecasting model on their own. The \textbf{objectives} of the project follow directly:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Digitize the SJCET weather register into a structured, traceable daily dataset.
    \item Validate the digitized values against the observers' own monthly totals and prepare them for modelling.
    \item Summarise the historical record: its coverage, rainfall and temperature.
    \item Develop a graph neural network that is pretrained on regional reanalysis, refined on nearby weather stations and fine-tuned on the SJCET register, and evaluate how much local data it needs compared with conventional forecasting baselines.
\end{enumerate}
